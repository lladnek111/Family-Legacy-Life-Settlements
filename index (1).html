      What is a life settlement?

      Most people think they have three choices. There may be a fourth.

      A life settlement is the sale of an existing life insurance policy to a third party for an agreed-upon payment. Depending on the transaction, the purchaser generally takes over future premiums and receives the applicable death benefit.

      Settlement proceeds may be greater than a policy’s cash surrender value, and are generally lower than its death benefit. Not every policy qualifies, and an evaluation is the only way to know.

      Your policy may be more than insurance

      Before you let a policy lapse, know what it may be worth.

      A life insurance policy you own is generally considered personal property. Like other property, it may be possible to sell it.

      The insurance company sets the cash surrender value according to your contract. A life settlement looks at the policy differently: licensed providers in the secondary market estimate what the future death benefit may be worth to them, and some are willing to pay more than the surrender value to own it.

      For a family weighing whether to surrender a policy or simply stop paying, that difference can matter. It can also turn out to be small, or there may be no offer at all. Knowing is what lets you decide well.

      Why values differ

      Two ways of measuring the same policy.

      The cash surrender value is what the insurance company will pay if you cancel the policy. It is set by the contract and does not account for the insured’s current health.

      A life settlement value is what a licensed provider may offer to become the owner. It reflects the death benefit, the expected cost of future premiums, the insured’s life expectancy estimate, and market conditions.

      Sometimes the gap between the two is substantial. Sometimes it is small, and some policies receive no offers. Results vary.

        Life insurance cash value and Medicaid

        Whether a policy affects eligibility depends on the program.

        A permanent life insurance policy may build cash surrender value over time. For Medicaid programs that apply an asset or resource test, accessible cash value may be considered a countable resource, depending on state rules and the characteristics of the policy.

        That is not true of every program. Many Medicaid programs based on Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) do not use an asset test at all.

        Generally no asset test

        MAGI-based Medicaid

        Most coverage for children, pregnant individuals, parents and, in many states, adults under 65 is based on income rather than assets.

        May involve resource rules

        Age, disability and long-term care

        Medicaid for people who are 65 or older, blind or disabled, and Medicaid for long-term-care services, can include limits on countable resources.

        Before surrendering for Medicaid

        Surrender is one way to make a policy liquid. It may not be the best one.

        Families sometimes own a policy that no longer fits the plan and may also affect Medicaid resource eligibility. Surrender pays the insurer’s cash value. A life settlement evaluation shows whether the market may pay more before the owner makes an irreversible decision.

        A policy may be worth a second look when it

        • Has significant cash value
        • Is expensive to maintain
        • Is no longer needed for its original purpose
        • May affect Medicaid resource eligibility

        An important planning concept

        The life insurance decision and the Medicaid decision should be coordinated.

        Selling or surrendering a policy turns an insurance asset into cash. Depending on the program and your circumstances, that cash may itself count as an asset for Medicaid purposes.

        Long-term-care Medicaid can also restrict transfers made for less than fair market value, including a five-year lookback period for certain applicants. Gifting or moving money without guidance can delay eligibility.

        For these reasons, families considering Medicaid should coordinate any policy transaction with a qualified Medicaid planning professional or elder law attorney before money moves.

        After a settlement or surrender

        More liquidity, more planning options.

        Once a policy’s value is liquid, a qualified professional can consider lawful, Medicaid-compliant ways to use or structure those funds that were not available while the value sat inside the policy. What is possible depends on your state, your program and your family’s circumstances.

        Family Legacy Life Settlements can coordinate with your Medicaid planner, elder law attorney, estate planning attorney or other qualified professional so the life settlement transaction fits into the broader plan.

        Options a planner may consider with liquid funds

        • Paying legitimate debts or expenses
        • Long-term-care costs
        • Home-related expenses
        • Medical expenses
        • Burial and funeral planning
        • Exempt or non-countable assets, where applicable
        • Planning for a spouse
        • Estate planning
        • Trusts or other legal tools, where permitted
        • Other Medicaid-compliant spend-down approaches

        These are topics to discuss with a qualified professional. Their availability and effect depend on state law and individual circumstances. No planning approach is guaranteed to preserve eligibility.
